# Ruth's Decision Patterns
*Started 13 August 2026. This document exists to capture recurring patterns in how Ruth actually makes decisions — not rules imposed on her, but observations of judgment already demonstrated across this project. Unlike WORKFLOW.md (process) or SELODIA_SPEC.md's Core Values (product principles), this is about the person making the decisions, and is meant to eventually outgrow this one project — likely extracted into its own standalone reference once there's enough real material to draw from, same treatment as the Development Workflow Principles.*

*Built incrementally, as real decisions happen — not written all at once. Each entry should trace back to an actual moment, not a general impression.*

## Observed patterns so far

**Precision over reassurance.** When a vague or comforting phrase is offered, Ruth consistently pushes for something clearer, even when the vague version is easier to accept in the moment. ("That's saved for you too, by the way" → rejected as unclear, even though it was gentler-sounding; "doesn't land on your legs" → corrected to the accurate physiological explanation rather than kept as a comforting myth.)

**Evidence over assumption, including correcting her own instinct.** Repeatedly asked for real research before accepting a claim, and was willing to have her own working assumption corrected by what the evidence actually showed (the recomposition-in-women claim, the exact whoosh mechanism, the joy-and-longevity framing narrowed to what was actually supportable).

**Resistance to anything paternalistic, even when well-intentioned.** Consistently caught and rejected persuasive or directive language, even in service of a good outcome (the MI-grounded language rules, "please reach out" flagged as an instruction, the guided-discovery onboarding design explicitly avoiding stated conclusions in favor of the user reaching them independently).

**Notices when something is technically correct but still feels wrong.** The clearest, most repeated pattern across the whole project — catching subtle experiential problems that pass functional testing (the vague-ack confusion, the mismatched grief/eating-disorder card, the silent-logging trust gap, the fourth-wall break). Usually surfaces as "that's not quite right" before being able to fully articulate why — the instinct arrives before the explanation.

**Willing to slow down and re-scope rather than ship something not fully stood-behind, even under real time pressure.** Held firm on proper safety investigation over quick patches multiple times, despite a genuinely tight timeline. (14 August 2026: with phase 4 done, chose to pull the MI guided-discovery activity flow out into its own dedicated later piece rather than let it be folded into the already-large onboarding phase as a follow-up — it "deserves the same dedicated attention as the safety language work, not to be folded into an already-large phase as an afterthought." The judgment is protecting the standard a piece of work is built to over the convenience of bundling it where it happens to be adjacent — and matching the depth of attention to the weight and sensitivity of the work, not to where it falls in the schedule.)

**Matches the strength of a guarantee to how much rides on the outcome.** When the correctness of something central is at stake, prefers an enforced mechanism over a well-intentioned instruction — willing to accept a little extra machinery to buy a real guarantee rather than trusting a softer approach to hold. (14 August 2026: on the MI guided-discovery gate, chose Option B — a route-enforced `readyToReflectLevel` field that structurally stops the deterministic level-echo from firing before the evocation has run — over the simpler Option A of just instructing the model to withhold the level, specifically *because* the discovery is the feature's whole value, so it warranted a route-level guarantee rather than trusting the prompt to behave. The reasoning was explicit: match enforcement strength to how much rides on the result. Note the contrast with the equipment step the same day, where button-driven capture plus AI-generated warmth was the right call — there, less rode on any single turn, so a lighter mechanism fit.)

**Prefers building real, working things over polished narrative before it's true.** Explicitly deferred marketing and content creation until the product itself is something she can honestly stand behind — "no point starting until I have a product I can stand behind."

**Designs for the degraded real-world state, not the ideal demo — and reframes the calmer choice as the stronger one, not a fallback.** Chooses the option that stays graceful when conditions are bad over the more ambitious one that only looks better when everything works. (18 August 2026: rejected a stroke-trace / line-drawing loading animation for the logo in favour of a gentle breathing pulse on the complete mark. The deciding reasoning was resilience under real conditions — a trace implies visible *progress toward completion*, so a stalled connection leaves a visibly broken, half-drawn mark on screen, whereas a pulse never implies completion and stays calm and whole even mid-stall. Explicitly named it as "genuinely the more on-brand choice under real conditions, not a fallback from the more ambitious idea." The pattern: judge a design by how it behaves in the failure case, and don't treat the quieter option as a compromise when it's actually the more robust one. Sits alongside "matches the strength of a guarantee to how much rides on the outcome" — both are about building for what actually happens, not the happy path.)

**Separates her own context from the product's requirements, and treats the leak as a defect to correct rather than a harmless example.** As the design-time example user, her own training context is the nearest source of concrete detail — which makes it the easiest thing to mistake for a product fact. She catches this and corrects it at the root. (19 August 2026: posterior-chain work had spread through the project's examples — the sample Almanac plan, the RDL safety note, a prominent group in the coverage workbook — and she named it plainly as her own training context, "not, and never was, a product focus for Unflump," then required that no requirement, coverage bar, acceptance test, or build assumption rest on it and that surviving references be explicitly labelled illustrative. The pattern is not modesty about her own preferences; it is recognising that a product built to serve "the full range of goals" is quietly narrowed every time the founder's own case is left standing as an unlabelled example. Note the verification instinct alongside it: the correction included checking whether code, schema, or migrations had absorbed the assumption, rather than assuming documentation was the only place it could have spread.)

**Scopes what an open question actually gates, rather than letting one unresolved item block everything adjacent to it.** When something is genuinely unresolved, she asks what specifically depends on it — and lets the rest proceed. (19 August 2026: the Romanian-deadlift variants were still unconfirmed when the $229 licensing decision came up. Rather than treating "an open item exists" as a reason to wait, she separated the two: the RDL preview governs the safety copy for *one movement*, while the purchase rests on broad movement-pattern coverage, which was confirmed. The open item stayed tracked as open rather than being absorbed into the purchase decision or quietly dropped to justify proceeding. The pattern is precision about dependency — neither over-blocking on an unrelated unknown, nor pretending the unknown is resolved because it is inconvenient. Sits opposite "willing to slow down and re-scope rather than ship something not fully stood-behind": both come from the same judgment, which is about locating what actually rides on a decision, not a general disposition toward caution or speed.)

**Attaches the abort condition to the authorisation, and stages the work so that using it stays cheap.** When approving a direction she is not certain about, she does not simply say yes and wait to see what happens. She names *in advance* what would make it wrong, delegates the judgment of whether that condition has been met, and requires it reported **before** anything is built. (31 August 2026: on adding a reputable-web-source layer to fill the gaps in the exercise classification data. The authorisation carried its own limit in the same breath — *"only use this if the sources can genuinely be trusted (established, reputable fitness/anatomy references, not arbitrary web content), and flag clearly if this introduces meaningful uncertainty or risk of pulling in unreliable information"* — alongside a separate instruction to *"report back honestly on feasibility and risk before implementing."* A three-exercise pilot found that two of the three returned muscle lists the reputable sources did not actually state: plausible content generated by inference and handed back **with citations attached**. She cancelled the layer outright and restated the reasoning in her own terms — it "produces confident-looking fabrication disguised as citation, which is worse than an honest AI classification," and it fails hardest exactly where the gap is, since the web documents well only the common exercises that would have been classified confidently anyway. Total cost of the wrong direction: nothing.)

Three things make this distinct from "evidence over assumption, including correcting her own instinct." That pattern is about *accepting* a correction once it arrives. This one is about **building the correction path in advance** — specifying the failure condition at the moment of approval, when it is free, rather than discovering it after the work exists, when it is not. The staging is what does the real work: separating "look into this" from "build this" is what made abandoning it cost nothing. And the reversal was total rather than partial — no attempt to salvage a reduced version of something that had just been shown to be unsound.

Worth noting what she did **not** do: she reaffirmed this direction twice before the pilot ran, over a recommendation against it. So this is not indecision or a weak commitment hedged in advance. The instruction was genuine and the abort condition was equally genuine, attached to it from the start — which is precisely what let strong commitment and fast reversal coexist without either being a performance.

**Will not decide on authority she cannot check — asks for the reasoning to be made legible first, then decides.** Presented with an expert recommendation, she separates the preference she already holds from the argument she has been given, and declines to act on the second until she can actually evaluate it. (31 August 2026: on the tagline typeface. Given a head-to-head and a set of trade-offs, she answered: *"I prefer sans as it all looks more coherent but I don't fully understand the implications you outlined."* Two things in one sentence — a clear preference, and a refusal to ratify it on borrowed reasoning. Once the three implications were restated plainly — that wide letter-spacing breaks words into loose letters at small sizes; that a light geometric sans can read as institutional, the one register the Brand Voice rejects; and that Comfortaa and Work Sans share a construction so the lockup reads as one type system — her own stated reason turned out to be the third of these, and the one a typographer would have led with.)

The tell is that this costs a round trip and she takes it anyway, rather than either deferring to the recommendation or overriding it on taste. It is adjacent to "resistance to anything paternalistic," but pointed inward: not *don't tell me what to do*, but *don't let me agree to something I can't independently assess*. Note also the sequence — the instinct ("more coherent") arrived before the vocabulary for it, which is the positive form of "notices when something is technically correct but still feels wrong": the same judgment running forwards instead of catching an error.

**Treats a generic answer as a failure even when it is not wrong — the test is whether the data already held the real one.** Noticing a leg-soreness question answered with "a heavier session in the last day or two" while 40 kg deadlifts and 90 minutes of ballet sat in the previous day's activity log, she did not read it as a wrong reply to be corrected. She read it as a *hollow* one, and turned it straight into a standing rule: a symptom query triggers a check of the prior 24-48 hours before anything is said.

The distinguishing move is the standard she applied. A reply that is defensible in isolation still fails if the app already stored what would have made it specific — "could not know" is forgivable, "did not look" is not, and from the person's side the two are indistinguishable. That is a higher bar than accuracy, and it is the bar an app whose proposition is *knowing you* actually has to clear.

She also recognised the shape rather than the instance: this is the Almanac insight rule (connect a result to a different data type across time) applied in real time, so it went into the spec as a general behaviour rather than a fix for soreness questions. Adjacent to "separates her own context from the product's requirements" — a lived annoyance became a product rule, not an anecdote.

**Names the class of evidence required before an irreversible step, not just that evidence is required.** Told that the Vercel rename gate was satisfied because the app was configured to call `api.selodia.app` and that domain resolved, she did not accept it: *"Need observed traffic, not just configuration evidence."* The rename went ahead only once the runtime logs showed five real POSTs arriving on that host and none on the old one. (3 September 2026.)

The distinction she drew is the whole of it. Configuration evidence says what *should* happen; observed evidence says what *did*. For a reversible change the first is plenty, and she has never asked for more. For a step that would break every installed build still calling the old host, she raised the standard specifically — and raised it before the step, not after it failed. The same move appears earlier the same day on the QuickLogBar: *"Investigate this first — report what's happening before fixing anything,"* where the suspected cause turned out to be wrong and the real one was worse.

This is adjacent to "will not decide on authority she cannot check," but it is pointed at proof rather than reasoning: not *explain it until I can evaluate it*, but *that is the wrong kind of proof for a thing this hard to undo*. It also runs in her own direction, not only outward — she carries unfinished work forward as Queued or Outstanding rather than letting a close-out record only what went well.

**Accepts a substandard state on purpose, and writes the expiry condition into the record at the same moment.** Told that a spoken turn measured ~4.4 seconds, she neither waved it through nor stopped to fix it. She dictated a spec note that did four things at once: stated the number, stated why it is tolerable *for this specific use* (“the use case is logging hands-free, not real-time conversation”), stated flatly that it is **“not permanently acceptable”**, and attached a figure to a named future event — *“under 3s before App Store submission.”* (4 September 2026.)

The move is the fourth part. A known-imperfect state that is merely accepted becomes permanent by default, because nothing in the record ever says when it stopped being acceptable — and the person best placed to notice is the one who accepted it, at the moment they accept it, when the reasoning is still fresh and free to write down. Binding it to App Store submission rather than a date is what gives it teeth: a date slips quietly, a gate does not.

Note what she did *not* do, which is the other half. She did not let the compromise expand to cover adjacent things: the same session’s optimisation work carried a standing instruction that **“the safety block stays untouched”**, repeated unprompted across three separate rounds of latency work. So the tolerance is scoped as tightly as the acceptance — this number, for this reason, until this event, and nothing else moves.

Adjacent to “attaches the abort condition to the authorisation,” and pointed differently. That pattern governs work whose *soundness* is uncertain, and names what would make it wrong. This one governs work already known to be below standard, and names what would make it no longer tolerable. Both are the same underlying instinct — write the limit down while writing it down is still cheap.

**Reads the actual requirement rather than the cautious version of it, and holds that reading when it is contradicted.** Told the ElevenLabs application video needed 60 to 90 seconds of the app running with voice, which made the whole submission wait on voice reliability that does not exist yet, she said plainly: *"the video is of me though, I can do it tomorrow at Wework"* and then, when that was not immediately accepted, *"the video of the app working must come later in the process."* She was right. The form asks for a short video introducing the founder, the product and the ElevenLabs use. Nothing in it requires a demonstration.

What makes this a pattern rather than a lucky call is that she had already conceded the point once, days earlier, when the same argument was made more confidently than it deserved. Coming back to it, restating it twice, and not being talked out of it the second time is the harder half. She also caught the next layer unprompted: *"i think less, it says max 3"* — a maximum is a ceiling, not a target, and eleven entries watched back to back reward brevity.

The general shape: **an overstated constraint quietly becomes a permanent blocker.** Three separate times in one day, a caution phrased as an absolute had to be walked back — the video dependency, "changing owner or slug would fork the project" when only the projectId matters, and a flat "turn analytics off" in place of the real argument, which was about timing. Each had been written down somewhere authoritative, and each was then being quoted back as a reason not to act. Her instinct in every case was to ask what the requirement actually says rather than accept the safe restatement of it.

The counterpart worth noting: she does not resolve these by overruling. She asks *why not*, repeatedly and without heat, until either the reason survives contact or it does not.

## How this gets built

At natural session-close points, add any new decision moment that reveals a genuine pattern — not every decision, only ones that add real signal about how Ruth thinks, distinct from the specific product or process choice itself.

**Sizes the response to a discovered mistake by what it actually changes, and locates the lesson in the timeline rather than in the act.** Told that 2,310 frames extracted from licensed clips had been sent to a model on 28 August, in plain breach of terms nobody read until 9 September, she did not escalate, remediate, or treat the finding as a crisis to be processed. She established the shape of it in one pass — no bad faith, the vendor had no muscle metadata so filling that gap was ordinary daily build work, and the licence was not read because there was no particular reason to think a formal one existed — and then decided: *"I say we leave everything as is and just keep going."* Nothing deleted, nothing disclosed, the manifest kept.

Three things make this a judgment rather than an avoidance. She separated **the method from the artefact**: the breach was in how the data was produced, so deleting 770 rows of real work would have un-analysed nothing and cost the project a month. She converted the finding into a **forward position** rather than a backward one — the animatics are "just a starting point," and the natural moment to raise this with the vendor is inside a commercial conversation about producing what Selodía actually needs, not as a standalone confession about a one-off classification pass. And she treated **the record as the remedy**: *"once we realised we documented it."* The thing that makes a good-faith breach defensible later is a contemporaneous account of what happened and why, which is exactly what was produced.

The tell is what she did not spend time on. No relitigating the decision to buy, no audit of what else might be non-compliant, no apology loop — and no pretending it had not happened either, which is the failure mode on the other side. Note the contrast with "willing to slow down and re-scope rather than ship something not fully stood-behind": that pattern spends time freely when the *quality of the work* is at stake. This one refuses to spend it when the outcome is already settled and the only thing left to buy is the appearance of having taken it seriously.

**Stops work that a pending decision might invalidate, rather than banking it as progress.** Offered a 750-row alias review that would visibly improve the demonstration coverage number, she declined it in the same breath as agreeing it was worth doing eventually: *"let's just keep building, we can review all this at 49, it's not holding us back"* — then, on being told the reasoning back, *"no point grafting for no reason, exactly."* Item 49 may replace most of the mobility library outright, and aliases curated against clips that get superseded are effort spent twice.

This looks like the RDL-scoping pattern above and is its mirror image. There, an unresolved item was prevented from blocking work that did not depend on it. Here, *available* work is refused because it depends on something unresolved — the same question asked in the other direction: not "what does this block?" but "what could make this pointless?" The distinguishing detail is that the deferred work was neither hard nor risky. It was simply downstream of a decision not yet made, and she declined to bank visible progress as a substitute for making that decision first.

**Holds anything in her name to the true size of the thing, and hears the difference between meaning and mechanism.** Across the ElevenLabs application on 11 September she corrected the copy again and again, and every correction pulled it towards what was actually true or actually felt, never towards what sounded stronger. Her story: *"I actually started strength training jan 2025"*, replacing a tidy "right as I turned 40" that was a year out. The product: "it isn't a diet app, a fitness tracker or a symptom checker" was *"not entirely true, it does those things"*; the point was *"holding the thread between all the parts."* The register: a closing answer was *"way too cocky and not my voice"*, and a birthday line was *"overkill."* A real app screenshot, flat weight after a hard session, came out because out of context it *"seems like a diet app"* and implied her goal was weight when it was body fat.

The second half recurred in the drafting. Twice she stopped copy that described *"what the code does"* (energy recalculation, smart-scale tracking, petals growing from the tip) and asked for *"the UX and the sentiment and philosophy"* instead. The Health Flower slide only worked once it said why six petals matter, not how they are drawn.

The shape: drafted copy drifts upward, towards bigger claims, a cleaner story and more mechanism, and she pulls it back to scale even when the inflated version reads better. An application whose whole strength is that everything in it is true treats a flattering inaccuracy as a cost, not a gain. This is the outward-facing twin of "reads the actual requirement rather than the cautious version of it": that pattern refuses overstated constraints, this one refuses overstated claims.

**Decides visual questions by comparison, and converges in small, named corrections.** Shown every recovered version of the Health Flower, she said plainly that none was the one she remembered, and did not settle for the nearest. Given variants side by side, she steered by deltas against what was on the page: *"ovals but thinner and more airy"*, then *"basically A but fatter in the middle ... but the colours of D"*, then *"between 1 and 2, slightly thinner tips and fuller middle"*, then *"too pointy, let's go with 1"*, then *"no, 2!"* Five rounds, each one a precise instruction relative to an option she could see. (11 September 2026.)

Two things make this a pattern and not indecision. She never tried to specify the design in the abstract; she needed options in front of her and answered each round with a comparison, which is faster and more exact than any description would have been. And her final reasons were brand values rather than taste, *"the nicest and lightest and most calm and minimalist"*, which is the brief for Selodía stated as a verdict. The practical lesson for anyone working with her on visuals: show a small labelled set, side by side, including the previous favourite for reference, and expect the answer as a delta.

**Orders the build by what each part is for: the destination before the channel that feeds it.** With voice needed for demo day on 21 October and next in the queue, she moved it behind the Almanac in one sentence: *"we move to almanac build before voice as that's what voice is for, to populate almanac properly."* The same day she held the weekly roundups, which the notification build had just made possible, because *"the current spec is too thin and the app has evolved significantly since early scoping"* and *"roundups land in the Almanac as weekly entries"*: the thing a roundup is written into had to be designed before the roundup. And when a good new idea surfaced mid-build (saved workouts appearing as quick taps, because *"gym could literally hold anything"*), it was recorded for Movement and the answer was *"stick with order."* (12 September 2026.)

What makes this distinct from "stops work that a pending decision might invalidate" is the question being asked. That pattern refuses work that sits downstream of a decision not yet made. Here both pieces of work were ready to start, and the choice was which comes first; she answered it by asking what each one is *for*, and built the part that gives the other its meaning. Voice without a well-shaped Almanac would be a fast way to fill a store that does not yet say anything; built the other way round, the voice work is aimed at a known target.

The tell is that it overrode a real deadline rather than yielding to it. Demo day made voice the obvious next thing, and the purpose order won anyway. Adjacent to "willing to slow down and re-scope rather than ship something not fully stood-behind", but pointed at sequence rather than standard: nothing about voice was below par, it was simply the wrong thing to perfect first.

**Refuses a scope reduction offered as a technical constraint, and sends the constraint back to be solved instead.** Told that the food cache could only short-circuit simple single-ingredient logs, because the parse handles whole messages and a name lookup cannot produce an itemised breakdown, she did not accept the narrowed version. She named what the feature was actually for and rejected the premise in one line: *"the cache scope needs to cover real food combinations, not just single ingredients. The whole point is remembering things like my chia pudding recipe or my usual cereal mix with their actual macros. If the routing can't handle that, the routing needs rethinking, not the scope."* (16 September 2026.)

She was right on the substance, which is what makes it a pattern rather than optimism. A composite somebody eats weekly is the *highest*-value thing to cache, not an edge case: it is precisely what the model has to re-reason every time and precisely where the answer is most stable per person. The narrowing would have shipped a feature that worked beautifully for bananas and never once for the food she actually eats. The same day she declined the spec's own shortcut in the other direction too — offered the option of skipping the McCance and Widdowson import as a prerequisite, the answer was *"build M&W properly as specified, don't skip it. Do it right."*

The distinguishing move is *where she pushes back*. A technical constraint arrives sounding like a fact about the world, and the easy response is to accept the reduced feature and call it scoped. She treats the constraint as a property of the current design rather than of the problem, and returns it. Adjacent to "reads the actual requirement rather than the cautious version of it", and pointed inward at the build rather than outward at an external requirement: there an overstated constraint was quietly becoming a permanent blocker; here an implementation difficulty was quietly becoming a permanent limit on what the product does.

Note the companion move on the same day, which shows the instinct is not simple persistence. Having approved AI-generated illustrations for fifteen movements the licensed library cannot cover — with a real condition attached, her sign-off on every image — she withdrew the approval within the hour on seeing a better route: *"Don't generate AI illustrations. Find a free SVG yoga/flexibility illustration library with a commercial use licence."* No sunk cost in her own decision, and no defending it because it was hers. So the pattern is not "hold the line"; it is that the standard belongs to the outcome rather than to whoever proposed it, including herself.

**Insists on a fault she can see, against a measurement that says there is none.** Told the Health Flower was not rendering, I measured every petal on her screenshot against the week's stored coverage, found them consistent to within a pixel, and reported that the drawing was correct and the week was simply sparse. She did not accept it: *"no, things were logged and the flower wasn't rendering. for some reason it's back, I thought you fixed it."* The fault was real and one line of code: the Overview refetched its three cards whenever the screen came back into view and never asked the flower's hook to read again, so the petals held whatever they had been at first mount and only corrected themselves when an app restart remounted everything. (17 September 2026.)

What makes this a pattern rather than luck is the kind of evidence each side had. Mine was a single frame, and a single frame cannot show staleness: correct-looking output and correct behaviour are indistinguishable in a screenshot. Hers was the sequence — logged, looked, nothing, restarted, there — which is the only place a refresh bug exists at all. The lesson is not "believe the user over the data"; it is that agreement between a render and its data proves consistency, not correctness, and a claim about *when* something changed cannot be checked in an artefact that has no time in it.

The same day she declined the diagnostic ping-pong it produced. Three rounds of screenshots failed to pin down where a voice note was breaking, each round costing her a trip to the phone; the answer was to make the app write its own failures to a table I could read, which found the cause on the next attempt. Both halves say the same thing about her time: she will describe what she saw once, precisely, and expects the next step to be instrumentation rather than another request for the same description.

It recurred on 19 September, and this time the fix had already shipped. Told that the log now re-read itself whenever she opened it, she logged a snack by voice, opened the log, and reported it missing again: *"i should need to close and open the app for the log to populate."* The re-read was real and worked on every row that already existed. What it missed was her order of events: a spoken meal is saved a few seconds after the reply, she went straight to the log to check, and so she arrived before the row did. I had tested the fix against the sequence I imagined; she tested it against the one she lives. The second fix re-reads again at four and ten seconds, which is only obvious once the timeline is hers rather than mine.

**Names the category of a fault, not the instance — which is what makes her feedback buildable.** Through a long UI pass on 18 September she never once reported a symptom on its own. The Movement tab was not "the text looks wrong", it was *"it's just two body text lines sitting under the segmented control. It doesn't feel intentional … it's not the wording that's missing now, it's the typography hierarchy."* The screens were not "too big", they were *"more like an accessibility layout than the calm, premium editorial aesthetic"* — which says what the excess is doing, not that there is excess. And the one element still out of place she identified by its species: *"the top control is now one of the few remaining elements that still feels like a standard mobile component rather than something bespoke."*

Each of those named a missing or wrong *class* of thing, and each produced a different build than the symptom would have. "The text looks wrong" produces a font change; "the typography hierarchy is missing" produces a reusable Section Introduction component used on every screen in the app. "Too big" produces a smaller number; "this reads as an accessibility layout" identifies that the fault is in the space *between* elements rather than around them, which is what the page margin had been wrongly blamed for twice.

The distinguishing move is the second sentence. She states what she sees, and then states what kind of thing is wrong — unprompted, and in the vocabulary of the discipline rather than of the screen. Compare "decides visual questions by comparison": that pattern is how she *converges* once options exist, and needs candidates in front of her. This one is how she *opens*, before anything has been drawn, and it is the more valuable half: a well-named category tells you what to build, where a comparison only tells you which of two things is closer.

The same day she demonstrated the cost of the alternative. Told the per-entry delete had shipped, she did not say it was wrong — it worked — she said *"the delete button should be easier to reach than clicking inside each one, this would be tedious to do for a user"*, which named the fault as **reach**, and produced a control on the row rather than a nicer dialog. A feature that works and is not worth using is a category she reports as a defect, and she reports it in those terms rather than as a preference.

**Asks for the way out to sit where the way in is, and applies the test at once to the next thing.** On 19 September she was shown the consent screen a second time, went to Settings to look at her answers, and found nothing there: *"I think this needs to be there as ppl may change their mind? Or not? What's best practice."* Told that UK GDPR says withdrawing consent must be as easy as giving it, and shown the new "Your choices" card, her next message did not comment on the card. It applied the same test to the neighbouring control: *"Same with step tracking, you be able to turn that off, no?"* — a permission that could be granted from Settings and never withdrawn from it.

Two things make this a pattern rather than a pair of requests. The first is the direction of travel: she did not wait to find the second instance, she went looking for it the moment the first was named, which is the category-naming habit above turned into a search. The second is the form of the question. Both times she asked rather than asserted, and the first time asked for the norm outright. She treats a missing exit as a defect of respect before it is a defect of design, but she wants the rule that says so, not just her own sense of it, before it becomes a requirement. The build consequence is the useful half: any setting somebody can switch on now gets checked for its off switch in the same place, and "turned off" has to mean the thing stops, not that a preference is stored while the reading carries on — which is what the step reader was doing.

**When a guard's unit of comparison carries no meaning, it cannot be patched — move the division instead.** The report's summary was checked by a whitelist: every number the app had counted was allowed, any sentence naming something else was deleted. It failed three ways in one review, and the three looked like separate bugs. `7 nights` in the facts let through *"slept 7 hours a night"*, *"woke 7 times"* and *"body fat is around 7%"*. A symptom reading *"lasted about 40 minutes"* let through *"migraines affected 40% of the days"*. A single date contributed 2026, 9 and 14 as separate integers, quietly permitting most small numbers in the language.

The instinct was to patch: match the unit as well as the digit, stop whitelisting free text, exclude date parts. Each would have narrowed one hole. None addressed the cause, which is that **a bare digit is not a claim** — what makes a figure right or wrong is its unit and its subject, and a set of numbers cannot express either. Every patch would have been a new approximation of a judgement the representation could not hold.

The app now prints the figures it counted, as their own block, and the summary states no figures at all — it says what the records show when read together, which is the part a model is actually good at. That is not a guard with bugs in it, it is a guard being asked a question its inputs cannot answer, and the next patch will be followed by another. It pairs with *a guard belongs at the write*: that one says enforcement must be in code rather than in a prompt, and this one says the code must compare something that means what you need it to mean. **A guard that deletes correct work is worse than no guard.** The same afternoon, before the review, the same checker split sentences on every full stop — so *"Her waist measured 74.5 cm."* became *"Her waist measured 74."* and *"5 cm."*, and both halves were deleted for naming figures that do not exist. Someone who sees a correct line vanish stops believing the checker, and after that the guard is decoration. **Nothing costs more than a feature that was never wired, because it looks exactly like one that works.** Nine faults across two weeks share a single shape, and once named it is visible everywhere. Not one of them errored. Not one logged anything. Testing does not catch these, because a test asks whether a thing does what it should and never asks whether the thing exists. Nor does using the app: an absent feature and a quiet one are indistinguishable from the outside.

The generalisable part is why it lands harder on a first build. These are not errors of logic, they are errors of **absence**, and absence is invisible to anyone who has not seen the thing present. An experienced developer opens the file tree and thinks *where is the provider that makes gestures work*; that list is what experience actually is. Two working rules came out of it. **A check that passes every day a bug is live is the wrong shape, not too small.** The drink-logging path had thirty-seven passing probes and a live check against the real model, and both passed on 21 and 22 September while a drink named alongside food was being dropped entirely. **A gesture may be the elegant way to do a thing; it may not be the only way.** Asked for swipe-to-delete, the codebase already carried the opposite decision in `row-delete.tsx`: *"Not a swipe: a hidden gesture is a control that only some people ever find."* Rather than silently overriding either her instruction or the file, both were kept: the swipe she asked for, and the visible Delete button that was already there, sitting beside it.

**Measure the fix before you build it, especially when everyone already agrees it is the right fix.** Streaming the voice reply had been the top build item for a week. What made it happen was asking "how much would this help" instead of "let's do it". **An argument list is not a dependency graph.** Loading a person's context ran as three batches, one after another, and the ordering looked necessary because each function took what it needed as parameters. `loadDayState(supabase, profile, dayStart)` clearly needs the profile, so it clearly has to wait for the profile query. The same was true of the batch above it, which needed nothing from the batch it was waiting on.

A function that takes a value as an argument looks like a function that cannot start without it. Often it only cannot *finish* without it. Splitting the fetch from the computation - `loadDayStateRows` and `buildDayState` - made the dependency real instead of implied, and the reads went out with everything else.

The tell is a sequence of awaits where each one reads plausibly but nobody has checked what the next actually consumes. Worth noting the same file had already been through this once: a comment dated the day before read "anything added here belongs in this batch, not after it", and the batch it described was itself sitting behind another one. Fixing the inner ordering felt like fixing the problem. (24 September 2026.)

**Before building a workaround, check whether the platform already does the thing and has it switched off.** Voice sometimes said "Bear with me a moment" before answering. That line was ours, written because a slow turn left people listening to silence, and it misfired often enough that she complained about it twice. The platform has had exactly this feature since before the app existed. And the platform runs automatic LLM cascading, so a failure of our custom model could have been answered by a generic one with none of the safety path in it.

Three findings, all from reading a vendor's own configuration and documentation rather than our code. The pattern is that a workaround is evidence of an unread manual more often than it is evidence of a missing feature, and the cost is doubled: the effort of building it, plus the worse behaviour of the version you built. When something in the product exists to paper over a third party's shortcoming, the first question is whether it is actually a shortcoming. (24 September 2026.)

**A renderer you can see is not the renderer she is using.** She reported the Almanac's segmented control drawing its selected panel as a square inside a rounded track. Three times I changed the corner radius, shot the Expo web preview, saw clean pills, and told her it was fixed. Three times it was still square on her phone.

Those are different renderers with different bugs, and the one thing a screenshot from localhost could never show is a fault that only exists in the other one. Worse, the clean render was read as evidence that the fix had worked, so each failure sent me looking for a reason the fix had not REACHED her - her font scale, then the update not having downloaded - and the second of those had her reopening the app to check something that could not change.

The fault was that the selected panel had no background colour until it was selected, so Android built its drawable at the moment of selection and built it without the radius. Any number would have been square. She diagnosed it in one sentence - *"Pill is fine until I select it and it reverts back to square"* - because she was describing BEHAVIOUR, and behaviour is what survives the gap between renderers.

Two general forms. When a visual bug is reported from a device and the preview renders it correctly, that gap IS the finding: the fault is in the native layer and every further theory about the values is wasted. **A closed list of intents will quietly discard whatever it has no name for.** She logged a thigh measurement, the chat acknowledged it warmly, and nothing was written. The writer underneath had handled waists, thighs and blood pressures correctly since the day it was built - its own instruction says to put any body measurement that is not weight, body fat or muscle into a personal metric. It simply never ran, because the only thing that calls it is a classifier choosing `measurement` from a fixed list, and that list's description defined a measurement as *"a weight, body fat percentage, or muscle mass"*. A thigh is outside that definition, so `none` was the obedient answer, and `none` runs nothing.

It failed intermittently rather than always, which is worse: a thigh genuinely IS a body measurement, so the model sometimes routed it properly in spite of the instruction. **When every rule you add makes the thing worse, the rule was never the fix.** The in-app chat answered noticeably worse than the same model asked the same question directly, and the natural reading was that the prompt needed work. It had had plenty: `GENERAL_CONDUCT` had reached 11,666 tokens, and every line in it was there because of a real incident. Reading the six incidents in order is what settled it. A reply had told her the almonds and the coffee were logged and the litre of water was in too, and the water had been lost that day: the rule added was several hundred words telling the model never to list back what went in, when the real fix was to tell it what had actually happened to the data. Five of six had the same shape. Something the model needed to know was missing, and instead of telling it, we told it how to behave without knowing. The rule never fixed the incident; it made the prompt longer, and every genuine instruction in there now had one more thing to compete with.

So the rebuild inverted the burden of proof. Six earned their way in, out of 11,666 tokens of predecessors, and the whole prompt came to 770. The general form: a prompt rule is the right fix only when the model has everything it needs and is still choosing wrongly. When it is choosing wrongly because nobody told it something, tell it - as a fact, not as an instruction. And a rule that has to argue is usually standing in for a fact that was never passed. **A test that cannot fail is worse than no test, because it reports success.** Twice in one evening the chat test suite printed PASS on a reply that plainly failed the thing being checked, for two unrelated reasons. Two checks had been written through a shell heredoc, which turned the word-boundary escape into a literal backspace character, so neither regex could match anything at all - and both passed on a reply that opened "Got it, 56.9 kg logged". Separately, one case's five checks were all negatives, each asking whether something wrong was absent, so all five passed on the reply "What's on your mind?". Nothing was there to be wrong.

Both were caught by reading the replies by hand, which does not scale and is not a guarantee. And the second-order finding is the one that matters: care was being taken on both occasions. Resolving to be more careful was not available as a fix, because carefulness had already been spent.

The guard went at the write instead. It found two more unfailable cases on its first run and a mangled regex on its second - neither of which anybody had noticed by reading them.

A third pattern came out of the same evening. Five checks turned out to be testing a turn of phrase rather than a behaviour: one demanded the literal words "3 readings" and failed a reply that said "Weigh-ins: 3", another demanded "saved" and failed "it's not in your log". Each had been written by copying a good reply's wording instead of asking what the reply has to achieve. When a check fires, read the reply before touching the check - and when the check turns out to be the wrong one, widen it to the behaviour and record the reply that exposed it. **One message, one author.** A reply could have up to eight separate notes appended to it after the model had finished writing: a save confirmation, a correction, a deletion, an honesty note about a failed save, an Almanac offer. Each was individually correct and each was written by the app, in the app's voice, beneath a paragraph written by the model that knew nothing about them. That is the entire mechanism behind the worst reply of the week, which told her the two coffees were "logged fine, no need to repeat it" and then, two lines down, "it looks like that entry didn't save for some reason. No prompt rule could have prevented it, because the model had already stopped writing before the contradiction was added. The fix is structural: the app does its saving first, and what it did goes IN, as labelled facts, before a word of the reply is written. Given both facts up front, the same model produced one coherent sentence about each.

The general form is that appending to a finished piece of writing makes a second author, and two authors in one message will eventually disagree. If the app knows something the reader needs, the model needs it before it starts, not the reader afterwards. The one exception kept deliberately: the Almanac offer is a question that must be asked exactly once, so it is recomputed against whatever reply comes back rather than passed in - the existing check already suppresses the app's line when the reply asks it already. **A layout cannot rescue a figure.** The weekly roundup needed a UI pass: four paragraphs in a chat bubble with every number buried in a clause. The obvious build was to split the prose into rows. **A step described in the documentation as automatic is a step nobody checks.** Nine sessions - 45 to 53, 16 to 24 September - are missing from the build log, and the close-out workbook has every one of those dates. Which was true of the old problem, and the correction created its exact opposite: a chore that reads as *already done*, every session, for work nobody was doing. The second shape is the more dangerous one, because a nagging step gets checked and a settled one does not.

**A constraint the database accepts is not a constraint the application can use.** A partial unique index was applied, verified, and correct. The client call that depended on it asked Postgres to ignore conflicts on that column, and Postgres cannot infer a partial index from an ON CONFLICT clause with no matching WHERE — so it raised an error on every call rather than only on a conflict. The route logs a failed write and carries on, deliberately, because a database problem must never cost somebody their reply. That deliberate kindness is what hid it for thirteen hours: the replies reached her phone and were thrown away, and the only trace was a log line in a place nobody was reading.

The mistake was not the index. It was verifying the migration and not the call site — checking that the thing exists rather than that the code can use it. The general form is the same as "a module built for a failure is not a module wired to it", one level further out, and the test is equally cheap: run the statement the application will actually run, once, against the real schema, before shipping. **A check on what was SAID is not a check on what was MEANT.** An allergy filter matched stored allergen names against the text of every reply, on purpose, ignoring the model's own claim about whether it was suggesting food — because a self-report can be gamed and a string comparison cannot. The reasoning about self-reports is right. What it gets wrong is treating "this reply mentions nickel" as "this reply suggests nickel", and those two come apart completely on exactly one subject: questions about the allergy itself. Every true answer to "is a nickel reaction common?" contains the word nickel, so every true answer was replaced with a refusal. The fix keeps the distrust and changes what a match means: a hit on a reply the model did not flag as suggesting food is now adjudicated rather than blocked, so a jailbreak gets judged instead of waved through and a real answer gets a correct verdict. The pattern to carry is that a deterministic backstop still needs to be a backstop against the right thing. Matching a word is cheap and unfoolable and answers a different question from the one being asked, and the same mistake had already appeared this week in test checks that asserted a turn of phrase rather than a behaviour. **Ask what the question is before reusing the function that answers a different one.** The weigh-in card compared her morning reading against one from six days earlier and called a 1.4 kg difference more than a single-day blip. **A status document drifts because checking it is a different job from updating it.** The standing list of open actionables carried three items marked "not started" that had shipped the same day the list was written, in commits written by the same person. **Measure the cost of the fix as well as the benefit, and measure it on real traffic.** A rebuilt chat path went live and the median time from message to reply doubled, 3.3 seconds to 7.0, measured from stored rows rather than from a probe. **A permission set correctly in one direction can be a gap in the other.** The waiting-list table is the only publicly writable one in the schema: insert is open to anyone, and there is no select policy at all. **A rule that says when to do something and not what happens afterwards will fail at exactly that seam.** A standing instruction — keep working until the queue is empty, do not stop to report — held for a full day of work and then did not. **Design for the person who will not fill anything in.** The beta feedback screen replaced three external forms that had collected nothing. A wrong test produces a result somebody can reproduce and argue with; an inference produces a confident sentence with nothing behind it, and the hedge ("as far as I can tell") reads as diligence while doing none of the work. **The rule: before writing that something does not exist, name the check that would have found it, and run that check.** If there is no such check, say that instead. (28 September 2026.)

**A derived value is only as good as the fact it is derived from, and the two are easy to confuse.** Her rule for the beta agreement's version date is "the day it is first shown to a tester", so rather than let somebody type a date in weeks later I wrote a script to read it from the database: the earliest row in `beta_members`. Granting somebody beta access is how they come to see the agreement, so the earliest grant is the day it was first shown. That reasoning is correct and the script was wrong, because the earliest grant was *hers* — made that morning so she could see the feedback screen she had just been given.

The mistake is not carelessness about the query. It is that **"who is in the beta" and "who has been shown the agreement" are different facts that overlap almost completely**, and the founder is the one row where they come apart. I reached for the column that was there instead of the fact I wanted, which is what deriving always tempts you to do — the available data is a proxy for the real thing, the proxy is right nearly always, and the exceptions are exactly the rows nobody thinks about.

The fix is one `.neq()`. The rule worth keeping is the question that would have found it before it ran: **name the rows where the proxy and the fact disagree, before trusting the proxy.** If the answer is "none", the derivation is sound. If the answer is a specific row, it belongs in the query. (28 September 2026.)

**Right about what the code asked for, wrong about the machinery underneath it — three times in two days.** The cost-per-turn table recorded nothing. The insert was started and deliberately not awaited, because recording a statistic must never put a database round trip in front of somebody's reply. That reasoning is right. What it missed is that a serverless instance is entitled to freeze the moment the response is sent, so the write began and then the machine running it stopped existing. Next.js has `after` for precisely this, and the fix was one line.

It is the same shape as the curl POST that returned 200 and wrote nothing, because a Server Action needs a header and an encoding the browser supplies. And the same shape as the latency probe whose fixture described a turn that could not happen. In all three the code was locally correct and the environment it ran in behaved differently from the mental model of it: a promise runs to completion, a POST is a POST, a fixture is a turn.

What makes this a family rather than three unrelated slips is that **none of them failed loudly.** The curl returned 200. The probe produced numbers. The insert threw nothing, logged nothing, and left an empty table that looked exactly like a quiet week. **A silent success is the signature of an environment assumption, and it is the one failure mode that testing the code alone cannot catch** — the code did what it said. The check that works is the one that asks the *other* system what it saw: read the table, open the page in a browser, look at the row. (28 September 2026.)

**A test that a broken implementation would still pass is not a test, and the sentinel is where it hides.** The pricing function got eight checks, and the run ends by executing all eight against a deliberately broken pricer that returns the same value for everything — if fewer than eight fail, the set is decoration and the script exits non-zero. The first version used `0` as that value, and correctly refused to pass: one case is "a call that did nothing costs nothing", which legitimately expects `0`, so it passed against the broken pricer.

That is a small bug in a guard and it is worth writing down because the guard caught itself. **The sentinel has to be a value no case can honestly hold** — `-1` for a cost that cannot be negative. The general form: when you prove a check set can fail, the thing you break it with must be outside the range of every legitimate answer, or you will quietly exempt whichever case sits on the boundary. And the boundary cases are the ones most worth checking. (28 September 2026.)

**A safety check that runs on a value something else then replaces is worse than no check, because the turn is recorded as checked.** The allergy gate ran on the reply the old chat path wrote. The condition that lets the rebuilt path take over asks whether that reply passed the gate. The rebuilt path then writes a completely different sentence and stores it. So for anybody with a declared food allergy, the gate was examining a draft that was thrown away moments later, and every reply that actually reached them was ungated.

Nothing about it is visible in the shape of the code. Both branches assign to the same variable; the gate sits above both; a reader following the flow sees a check and then a reply. It survived a typecheck, a lint and a day of use, and the only reason it surfaced is that streaming forced the question "at what moment is this reply final?" — which is the same question the gate should have been asked.

**The general form: when a value is checked and then reassigned, the check belongs to the reassignment, not to the variable.** Worth applying wherever a pipeline has a validated value and a later stage that can substitute a new one — a sanitiser followed by a formatter, a permission check followed by a retry with different arguments. The question that finds it: *which of the things that could end up in this variable have been through the check, and which have not?* If the answer is "the first one", the check is in the wrong place. The structural guard is in `scripts/check-gated-paths.mjs`. (28 September 2026.)

**A table created in a hurry, for a good reason, skips every habit a real table gets.** Before deleting duplicate chat rows, they were copied into a backup table. That instinct is right and it is the one the close-out ceremony asks for. What it skipped is that a table created in `public` is served by PostgREST, and without row-level security the anonymous key reads it — the key that ships inside the mobile app and inside the landing page's own JavaScript. For a day, a copy of somebody's conversations about her body was readable by anybody who looked.

The table had a comment explaining why it existed. It was made carefully. **What made it dangerous is precisely that it was not a feature**: a feature gets a migration review, an RLS policy, a line in the spec. A backup table gets made in the same minute as the deletion it protects, by somebody thinking about the deletion.

It was found by a security advisor run while looking up something else entirely, which is not a process. **The rule: a table is a table. If it is created in a schema the API serves, it gets row-level security in the same statement that creates it**, and something runs afterwards that tries every exposed table with the public key — because the question is not "is RLS enabled" but "can that key read this". `scripts/check-rls.mjs`. (28 September 2026.)

**A gap in a timeline is not automatically an opportunity, and the difference is where the work sits inside it.** Streaming the spoken reply was estimated at about 1.9 seconds, from a measurement: the saves confirm a median 1.9s before the reply writer emits its last token. The estimate was honest and it was wrong. It measured almost nothing — 124ms — because that 1.9 seconds is not the model producing text steadily; it is mostly the model producing nothing yet. There was no early text to speak because none had been written.

The fix was to cut at a clause rather than a sentence, which took the measured benefit to 680ms median and 2.5s at best. But the lesson is the estimate, not the fix. **A duration between two events tells you how long something took, not how its work is distributed inside it**, and a plan that depends on the distribution needs the distribution measured rather than inferred from the total. The tell was available and unasked: nobody had measured when the first sentence appeared, only when the last one did. (28 September 2026.)

**Premium means restraint, and a day inside a competitor's funnel is what made it a rule.** BetterMe's checkout offered 61% off at six in the evening and 71% off at seven, same plan, same account, because the sale had not closed. A promo code carried the user's first name. A countdown ran throughout. The quiz she completed asking for *calisthenics* returned "your goal: reach 52 kg" on a screen whose own previous page had told her her BMI was normal, then "lose weight" in the follow-up email, then "maintain weight" with a target six kilos below her actual weight at checkout. The "as featured in" logos were one set of publications on one page and a different set on another.

None of that is incompetence. Every piece of it works, which is why it is everywhere, and it is precisely why the rule has to be written down before there is any commercial pressure to break it. **A discount that deepens because somebody hesitated is not a discount. A goal the user did not set is not personalisation. A countdown that resets is not urgency.**

THE RULE, and it is absolute rather than a preference:
- **No escalating or personalised discounts.** The price is the price. The only reduced price is the founding rate: real, capped at 100, honoured for good.
- **No fake urgency.** No countdown timers, no "offer ends", no scarcity claims. A body-literacy app has no reason to be urgent, and manufacturing a reason is the tell.
- **Email is what the user needs or asked for.** At most one gentle follow-up to somebody who started signing up and stopped, sent once, a day later, no discount and no pressure. No marketing email without explicit opt-in. One-tap unsubscribe that works.
- **No "as featured in" unless it is true, specific and linked.**
- **No body images in marketing or onboarding.** Any kind.
- **Goals are the user's words and are never rewritten into weight loss.**

The test to apply to anything new: *would this still be here if it did not increase conversion?* If the honest answer is no, it is a dark pattern wearing a design. (28 September 2026.)

**Goals are welcome, shame is not.** The product has drifted into vagueness about goals, and the drift came from a good instinct: Selodía exists against the culture of making women smaller, so naming a fat-loss goal felt like joining it. That is the wrong conclusion and it makes the app less useful to the person it is for.

A woman who says she wants to lose body fat should be helped to do it precisely - real targets, real numbers, the reasoning shown. **What Selodía refuses is everything that comes after the goal**: the before-and-after, the target nobody asked for, the progress bar, the implication that she was a problem to be solved.

The distinction is between a goal and a verdict. **Refusing to name a goal is not kindness; it is the app being unable to help**, and the user notices, because she came with something specific in mind and left with a maintenance target. (28 September 2026, and the evidence is that onboarding sets no focus state at all - see the audit.)

**A default that silently stands in for a missing answer will be mistaken for an answer.** Onboarding never writes the fat or muscle focus, and the function that reads them is `(v) => (v === 'reduce' || v === 'increase' ? v : 'maintain')`. Nothing is null downstream, nothing errors, nothing logs. Every user simply comes out on a maintenance target, including the one who spent five minutes explaining that she wants to lose body fat.

What makes this hard to see is that the default is *reasonable*. Maintenance is the safe middle, and a reviewer reading that line nods. **The bug is not the value, it is that "she chose maintain" and "nobody ever asked her" produce identical state**, so no query, no dashboard and no test can tell them apart afterwards.

The rule: **when a missing answer and a real answer collapse to the same value, keep them apart in the data even if they behave the same in the code.** A nullable column, or an explicit `unset`, costs nothing and preserves the only question worth asking later - did anybody actually choose this? The same shape is everywhere: a timezone that defaults to UTC, a currency that defaults to GBP, a role that defaults to viewer. (28 September 2026.)

**A guard that only says "keep going" is a trap, so build the ways out first and prove each one.** The overnight stop hook blocks the end of a turn while work remains, and it writes to the progress file, which means a bug in it could have run unattended until morning. Three escapes went in before it was ever enabled: a file whose presence disables it, a ceiling on continuations, and an item that has not moved across three stops being marked blocked so the run passes it.

**And it fails open.** Every error path exits silently and lets the turn end, because the failure mode of a stuck hook is much worse than the failure mode of a hook that stops working.

The part worth generalising is the testing, not the valves. Each one was proved against a throwaway copy of the repository layout rather than the real one, so the test could exercise the destructive path - marking an item blocked, writing to a progress file - without any chance of touching the real thing. **When a mechanism's job is to keep something running, its tests have to be about stopping.** (28 September 2026.)

**A check that matches a phrase is testing wording; a check that matches an intent is testing behaviour.** The chat test set had `does not ask her to retype`, written as a search for "re-enter", "type it again" and two others. It failed a reply that said *"No need to type it again"* - which is the app doing precisely the right thing, in the words the check was hunting for.

A negative check is where this hides, because the failure looks like a pass in reverse: the reply was good, the score said bad, and the temptation is to soften the reply until the check is happy. **That direction of fix is the real cost** - it trains the prompt to avoid a word rather than avoid a behaviour, and the word was never the problem.

The fix was a negative lookbehind and six sentences to prove it, including the one that broke it. The rule is simpler than the regex: **when a check fires, read the sentence it fired on before changing anything else.** If the sentence is good, the check is wrong. (28 September 2026.)

**One broken stand-in only catches one direction of wrongness.** Three check files tonight ran every case twice - once against the real implementation, once against a deliberately broken one - and all three came back with checks marked WEAK. The pattern was identical each time and I did not see it until the third: **the broken stand-in removed nothing, so every check about NOT over-removing passed against it trivially.**

A gate can be wrong in two directions. The rules gate can fail to exclude a contraindicated movement, or it can strip a session of things that were fine. The red-flag matcher can miss chest pain, or it can tell a woman asking a general question to call an ambulance. **A stand-in that is only too permissive proves nothing about the half of the failure space that is too eager** - and in this codebase the eager half is the one that has actually bitten, twice: the allergy gate blocking two honest answers about nickel, and the first red-flag matcher firing on "is chest pain always serious?".

So: two stand-ins, one for each direction, and a check earns its place by failing against **at least one**. Everything left over is a regression guard or a sanity check, and those get their own heading and are counted separately rather than folded into the total. **A check that cannot distinguish a working implementation from a broken one is not evidence, and a summary line that counts it as evidence is the self-flattery the whole exercise exists to prevent.** (28 September 2026.)

**When the exclusion list is unbounded and the inclusion test is small, invert it.** The red-flag matcher needed to tell "I've got chest pain" from "is chest pain always serious?". The first attempt was a list of question openings - "is it", "what causes", "can you get" - and it failed on the very first sentence tried, because that one begins with none of them. The fix was not a longer list.

**Every real report of a symptom contains a first-person reference, and a question in the abstract contains none.** One rule replaced twenty, and it does not need extending each time somebody phrases a question a new way. The tell that a list is the wrong shape is that adding an item feels like it will be the last one and never is.

The same shape catches the allergy gate's original failure at one remove: **a MATCH is not a REPORT, exactly as a MATCH was not a SUGGESTION.** Both are filters that can find a word and cannot tell who is being talked about, and both were fixed by asking a question about the *sentence* rather than adding to a list of *words*. (28 September 2026.)

**A fabricated default lives in more places than the one you find it in.** The maintenance-target bug was reported as a line of TypeScript: `asFocus` turning null into `'maintain'`. Fixing that line would have changed nothing, because the column underneath was `NOT NULL DEFAULT 'maintain'` and the value was being written at row creation, before any code read it.

It was in three places - the schema, the server, and the app - and the schema was both the deepest and the least likely to be looked at, because a migration written months ago does not appear in a code review of today's bug. **All three accounts in the database carried it, including the founder's**, set to maintenance while she trained for a muscle up.

The rule: **when a value appears where nobody chose it, check the schema before concluding you have found the cause.** A `DEFAULT` clause is a decision somebody made once and nobody has read since, and it outranks every line of application code that comes after it. (28 September 2026.)



**A tool that drives the real app against real data must be unable to write, not trusted not to.** The screenshot script signs in as Ruth and clicks things by matching label text. It answered the Gym cadence question that was hers to answer, took Gym off two days, and moved an activity to Sunday. I restored all three, tightened the selectors, and that would have been the whole fix - a more careful click.

The rule was already written down in this repository, in check-food-parse.mjs: *"it is her production database, and a verification that changes the thing it is verifying is not a verification."* That file stubs the client so a write is impossible. The screenshot script had the same access and no such guard, because it felt like a camera rather than a client.

So the fix is not better selectors. Every mutating request to Supabase is aborted at the network layer during a shoot, and the run prints what it tried to send. The printout turned out to be the useful half: a later run reported a blocked PATCH, and that line was the proof the drag had completed end to end - a thing no screenshot could show. **A guard at the write is cheaper than the care it replaces, and it tells you things care never would.** (29 September 2026.)

**A substitution you make quietly becomes a bug report she has to write.** Ruth asked for long-press and drag. A cross-container drag is genuinely hard to verify without a device, so I built the hold as a shortcut to a chooser sheet instead, and wrote the trade-off at the top of the file in four careful paragraphs. Then I told her: *"Hold a card and move it to another day."*

She read that as drag, because it is drag. She tested it, it did nothing, and she spent a message diagnosing a feature that had never been built - asking, reasonably, whether it needed a native module her build was missing.

The comment was not the problem; the comment was good. **The trade-off was recorded where only I would read it, and described to her in words that hid it.** A decision to build something smaller than what was asked is hers to accept or refuse, and she can only do that if the message that ships it says so in the first sentence. The test is simple: if she would be surprised to learn what the code actually does, the summary is wrong however true each of its words is. (29 September 2026.)


**A frame you hand a model is a frame it will use.** Ruth asked why a card had not appeared and was told: *"That's on me for saying it would show up. I don't control that, only the app does. I'm not able to check what's actually stored there."* Her objection was not that it was unhelpful. It was that she IS the app, so hearing it wash its hands of her data destroys the thing the whole product rests on.

Three prompt rules, written weeks apart, each said some version of *"the app shows its own save confirmation"*. Every one of them existed to stop a receipt, and every one of them was right on its own. Together they had taught the model that there was somebody else in the room, and when it was cornered by a question it could not answer, it pointed at him.

The instinct was to add a rule forbidding the sentence. The actual fix was to remove the frame: all three reworded to speak about her screen rather than about a third party, and a check that fails the build if any rule in the prompt says "the app". **A model does not distinguish between the rules you wrote for it and the world those rules imply.** Watch for the noun you keep using in passing. (30 September 2026.)

**Retest the limit before you repeat it.** For several days I told Ruth I could not look at the app's screens, from one true observation: the Expo web dev server dies on this machine, four times now. That sentence hardened into "the screens cannot be seen", and from there into a reason every UI change had to be checked by her, on her phone, one round trip at a time.

The machine has 16 GB of RAM, 5 GB free, and a 21 GB pagefile that has never been more than a third used. It was never short of memory. The dev server re-bundles the whole app on every request and holds it, which is a shape of work that dies at any ceiling. `expo export` is a batch job and completes in four minutes.

The cost was not the four minutes. An onboarding screen shipped with no Continue button on it and went six days unnoticed, because looking at it had been ruled out by an inference nobody re-ran. **A limit inferred from one failure is a hypothesis, and it keeps its authority long after the evidence for it has stopped applying.** When a constraint starts shaping how the work is organised, that is the moment to test it again rather than the moment it becomes settled. (30 September 2026.)

**Collected, stored, and read by nobody is a class of bug, not an incident.** Three times in one day: the Me tab cards were built by one code path and never passed to the model that answers her; `life_stage`, `life_stage_detail` and `hrt` were gathered by nine careful onboarding questions and read nowhere on the server; and calcium turned out to be published in the government spreadsheet the import was already open in, one column along from the sodium it did take.

Each looked like its own small oversight. Together they are a shape: the collecting is visible, satisfying work with a screen at the end of it, and the reading is a line in a file nobody looks at. Nothing fails when the second half is missing. The feature simply behaves as though the person never answered.

So the rule is a commit-level one rather than a review-level one. **Whatever is stored gets read in the same commit that starts storing it, or it does not go in.** The check is not "did I wire it up" but "can I point at the line that consumes this, today". (30 September 2026.)

**A true sentence that changes nothing still costs attention.** A brief asked three times for a gentle note that tea and coffee reduce calcium absorption. The mechanism was crossed - the interaction that matters is with iron - and on calcium the effect is real, small, and in the source's own words *"easily offset by increasing calcium consumption in the diet"*, which is the habit the feature already teaches.

The tempting fix was to soften it: "may slightly reduce". That keeps a sentence which is defensible, unfalsifiable and useless, and spends a nudge on asking somebody to reorganise her morning for a benefit her next yoghurt erases.

In a product whose whole method is quiet repetition, attention is the scarce resource and every nudge is drawn from the same account. **The question is not "is this true" but "is this the smallest thing that would still change what she does".** Deleting it was the feature working. (30 September 2026.)
